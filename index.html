Founders & origins

From a long walk out of Italy.

Pascal Difazio left central Italy for England, walking much of the way. He landed at Falmouth and made his way to Frome. In 1914 he opened a bicycle shop at 25 Catherine Street. His son John was born the same year.

Motorcycles soon followed, arriving by train from the Midlands factories: BSA, Triumph, Brough. After the war, young Richard collected new bikes from Frome station on the shop’s “works” BSA and float sidecar.

John, known as “Jack”, began grass-track racing at 16 on a BSA Blue Star, then a Rudge Ulster. In 1947 he left a job at Pinks in London to take over the family business.

Dealer of note
  • British agencies: BSA, Triumph, Ariel, Velocette, Norton, Francis-Barnett, James
  • Honda from 1962 (via Len Meredith’s, Bristol), Suzuki 1963, Yamaha 1965
  • From the 1980s: a leading independent BMW twin specialist

The big idea

Steering from inside the wheel.

Telescopic forks steer, suspend and brake all at once, so they flex and dive. Jack split those jobs. A swinging arm handles suspension, and the wheel pivots on a king-pin inside its own hub.

“Up to 80mph the handlebars were almost superfluous… One could almost indulge in secondary pastimes like glancing at a map or eating a sandwich.”University of Manchester, 500-mile test
“The Difazio is the best overall machine currently available for high speed touring.”Same report, conclusion
Patent GB1274441
  • Filed 2 July 1968 by John Difazio
  • Inner hub on a king-pin, outer hub carrying twin discs
  • First hubs cast iron; later turned from solid aluminium billet

Notable machines

Built in an old abattoir.

Behind the shop, in a former abattoir, Jack and a team of three (Pat Hooper, Trevor Burridge and Tim Tucker) made the frames, turned the hubs, laid the fibreglass and assembled every machine.

1968 · Racer

AJS 7R special

The first solo hub-steerer, built entirely in-house around an ex-Hailwood AJS 7R engine. Richard raced it at Thruxton and Cadwell Park.

Photo: Difazio family archive / The Classic MotorCycle, courtesy of Classic Bike Hub — with thanks.

1969 · Road

Triumph Trident

The first hub-steered road bike, seen on Frome’s streets in autumn 1969. Four Tridents were eventually converted.

c.1970 · Road

Suzuki 500 “EYB 999J”

Road-tested by several journalists, then sold to a customer in the USA.

1971 · Kevin Charles

BSA Rocket 3

The only Rocket 3 hub-steerer. Kevin sold it in 1977 as a deposit on his first house and bought it back 18 months later.

1973 · Roger Margetts

Suzuki GT550

Built from a written-off GT550K, first owned by an RAF fitter, and featured in Bike in 1974. It sold at auction in 2005 for £1,500, with 17,000 miles on it.

Photo: BIKE ME!, courtesy of bikeme.tv — with thanks.

1973–74 · Norton

Norton Commando

An Andover Norton press bike converted for Norton’s Mike Jackson. Its owner says the Norton board learned of the project afterwards.

Photo: Tim (forum member “difazio”), courtesy of Access Norton forum — with thanks.

1975 · Endurance

Mead & Tomkinson “Nessie”

Difazio supplied Mead & Tomkinson’s first two hub-centre chassis. Their Laverda 1000 racer, with adapted Difazio steering, finished 10th at the 1976 Barcelona 24 Hours.

Photo: Bonhams, courtesy of Bonhams lot 417 (2007) — with thanks.

1975 · Complete bike

Ducati 750GTE

One of only two complete Difazio motorcycles, with a rare electric-start roundcase engine. It sold at Bonhams for £12,650 in 2019.

Photo: Bonhams, courtesy of Bonhams lot 386 (2019) — with thanks.

1976 · Kevin Charles

Triumph Hurricane

One of three built that spring. It won the Kay Engineering Trophy at Stafford in 2012.

1985 · Dave Ayesthorpe

Honda Gold Wing

One of two hub-steered Gold Wings, with over 100,000 miles covered. A second hub went on after a crash in 1987.

1970s · John May

BMW R90S

It came with a huge Falcon touring fairing that took two people three hours to remove.

2007/8 · Richard

BMW R65 Difazio Special

The last of five lightweight 750–854cc roadsters built by Richard, this one for himself.

The team

Four people, one workshop.

Jack was the designer, but making the machines took a team of four: Jack, Pat Hooper, Trevor Burridge and Tim Tucker. Between them they made the frames, turned the hubs from aluminium billet, laid up the fibreglass and assembled about 50 machines in the old abattoir behind the shop. Andy Westlake called it “very much a cottage industry if ever there was one”.

Tim Tucker

Tim built machines himself: a Suzuki 750 in 1974 that went to Canada, and in 1975 a BMW that went to Australia. He also built bikes that ended up in the USA.

Decades later he was still looking after them. Through the Difazio Motorcycle Interest Group he tracked down lost bikes, helped bring a Suzuki 500 back from the USA for restoration, and asked owners for photos “to add to the records”.

“I am the only one around from the original Team that built these bikes but have great enjoyment helping towards restoration and attending shows.”Tim Tucker, 2014
“It's the only bike of ours in Australia so it is important to us to locate it.”Tim, on the BMW he built in 1975

On track

Two generations of racers.

Jack raced a Rudge Ulster before and after the war, then solo and sidecar BSAs. He raced the 1956 hub-centre BSA outfit with passenger Brian Sherwood and retired from racing when Richard began. The Body brothers bought the outfit, and Henry Body still had the chassis 47 years later.

Richard joined the shop in 1957 and spent two seasons as his father’s grass-track passenger. From 1959 he road-raced a Gold Star, then Jack-prepared Manx Nortons, then a hub-steered Suzuki twin. That added up to 13 seasons and many National podiums.

Test day

The Suzuki-engined racer wouldn’t run straight on windy Westonzoyland airfield until Jack set the trail to 22°. Suzuki supplied “Daytona spec” tuning tips.

Little-known Difazio

Surprising facts.

Built in a slaughterhouse

The hub-steerers were made in a former abattoir behind the shop at 25 Catherine Street.

Car parts inside

The 1956 prototype used a Reliant-type swinging arm and a Ford Prefect brake drum.

Sketched on an envelope

The solo design came to Jack on a Sunday drive near Stonehenge, scribbled on an old envelope.

Told one disc was enough

Lockheed said “a single disc is the ultimate for any motorcycle”. Jack fitted two anyway.

A legendary visitor?

Jack remembered George Brough and T. E. “Lawrence of Arabia” visiting the shop in the mid-1920s. That memory is the only source.

“Quite a stir”

The Motor Cycle, 26 June 1968, on Richard’s AJS 7R hub-steerer’s debut at Thruxton.

Norton’s board didn’t know

Norton’s Mike Jackson had a press-fleet Commando converted in Frome. Its owner says the board only found out afterwards.

The Flying Banana

Royce Creasey’s feet-forward Creasey-Difazio CX500 went to the 1981 Isle of Man TT and lapped the course in 33 minutes on open roads.

From Frome to Grand Prix

Mead & Tomkinson’s “Nessie”, with adapted Difazio steering, encouraged the Elf team’s 1980s endurance and GP racers.

11th in the Senior TT

Michael Tryphonos raced a prototype based on the Difazio system at the Isle of Man, reaching 11th in the Senior TT.

Not new, but revived

Hub steering dates back to a 1910 James and the 1920s Ner-a-Car. Jack’s work drove its 1970s revival.

Exported worldwide

Team member Tim Tucker built bikes that went to the USA, Canada and Australia. One BMW owner called his “the best handling boxer twin I ever owned”.

A Laverda for his 90th

In February 2004 Andy Lloyd readied the only road-going Laverda triple with a Difazio front end for Jack’s 90th birthday.

“Always incontinent”

An ex-employee recalled that the hub oil seal always leaked, so owners topped it up through a ¼-inch screw hole between the spokes.

Still cited in 1990

European patent EP0432107B1, filed in 1990, cites Jack’s 1968 patent GB1274441 as prior art.

A Belgian tribute

Bernard Mont spent seven years building a Ducati 900SS “TT3 Difazio”, imagining a collaboration between Fabio Taglioni and Jack.

Legacy

A fitting tribute.

About 50 hub-steerers were built between 1968 and 1983, and roughly half survive. BMW and Norton both showed interest, but no manufacturer took the idea up.

When the old workshops faced redevelopment in the early 1990s, former employee John Ransome saved the moulds, hubs and spares. With Jack’s help he built recreations of a 500cc Suzuki racer and the Laverda “Nessie”.

In 2012, Kevin Charles’s 1976 Hurricane-engined Difazio won the Kay Engineering Trophy at Stafford. John Cooper, the former BSA works racer, presented it. Jack Difazio died the same year. Hub-centre steering later reached production on the Bimota Tesi, Yamaha GTS1000 and Vyrus. Those trace back to Elf and James Parker’s RADD designs rather than directly to Jack’s.
